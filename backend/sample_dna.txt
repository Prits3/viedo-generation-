§1 First isolation (1869–1919)
In 1869 Friedrich Miescher isolated a phosphorus-rich substance from the nuclei of white blood cells, which he called nuclein. Phoebus Levene later identified its components: a sugar (deoxyribose), a phosphate group, and four bases — adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C). Levene proposed the tetranucleotide hypothesis, that the four bases appeared in equal amounts in a simple repeating unit. This proved incorrect, and it made many researchers suspect proteins, not DNA, carried the genes.

§2 Chargaff's rules (1950)
Erwin Chargaff measured the base composition of DNA from many species. Within a given organism's DNA, the amount of adenine is approximately equal to thymine (%A ≈ %T), and guanine is approximately equal to cytosine (%G ≈ %C). Overall base composition varies between species. These findings contradicted the tetranucleotide hypothesis and hinted that the bases pair, though Chargaff did not propose a structure.

§3 X-ray diffraction evidence (1951–1952)
At King's College London, Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling produced high-quality X-ray diffraction images of DNA fibres. In May 1952 they recorded Photograph 51, whose X-shaped pattern is characteristic of a helix. Franklin's measurements indicated a regular helix about 2 nm wide, with the phosphate backbone on the outside.

§4 The double-helix model (1953)
In Cambridge, James Watson and Francis Crick built a model consistent with the X-ray data and Chargaff's rules. Published in Nature on 25 April 1953, it had two antiparallel sugar-phosphate backbones winding into a right-handed double helix, with bases paired inside: A with T by two hydrogen bonds, G with C by three. The helix is about 2 nm in diameter, roughly 10 base pairs per turn, with a rise of about 0.34 nm between bases and 3.4 nm per complete turn. They wrote that the pairing "immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material." They suggested, but did not prove, how DNA is copied.

§5 Confirmation, credit and caveats
In 1958 Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl showed replication is semiconservative: each strand templates a new partner. Franklin's unpublished data and Photo 51 were shown to Watson at the time without her knowledge. Franklin died in 1958; the 1962 Nobel Prize went to Watson, Crick and Maurice Wilkins and, by convention, is not awarded posthumously.
